BASELINE

HOW USD PRINTING WORKS

When the Fed "prints," it doesn't run physical presses.

The dollar supply expands through the banking system — and the way it expands determines whether the rupee gets a boost or a beating.

STEP 1

The Fed creates reserves

The Federal Reserve buys US Treasuries or mortgage-backed securities from banks. To pay for them, it credits those banks' reserve accounts. New dollars exist that didn't before — this is "quantitative easing" or QE.

2008–2014: ~$3.6 trillion added via QE1, QE2, QE3
2020–2022: ~$4.8 trillion added in COVID response
STEP 2

Banks lend it out

With more reserves, banks can issue more loans. Each loan creates a new deposit — which is broad money (M2). One dollar of central bank money supports many dollars of credit money. The supply multiplies.

M2 in 2000: $4.9 trillion
M2 in 2026: $22.7 trillion (+360%)
STEP 3

Dollars search for yield

Cheap dollars flow toward higher returns — Indian equities, Indian bonds, emerging market debt. This is the "carry trade." Rupee strengthens temporarily as foreign capital floods in.

2003–2008: Sensex up 6×, rupee strengthens to ₹39
2010–2013: EM bond inflows hit record highs
STEP 4

Then the music stops

When the Fed signals tightening — or when global risk rises — that same capital reverses. Investors sell rupee assets, buy dollars, wire money home. The rupee collapses harder than it had risen.

2013 taper tantrum: Rupee crashed from ₹54 to ₹68 in months
2022 hiking cycle: Fed +525bps, rupee broke ₹80 first time
FACTORS AT PLAY

Six variables that decide whether the rupee bends or breaks.

1

US monetary policy

QE → rupee strengthens · QT → rupee weakens

The Fed's balance sheet is the single biggest driver. When it expands, dollars flood emerging markets. When it shrinks, dollars retreat to safety. Indian rates can't compete with US rates on a risk-adjusted basis.

2

Crude oil prices

+$10/barrel → ~₹0.50 rupee weakness

India imports 85% of its crude. Every barrel must be paid in dollars. A $10 rise in Brent adds roughly $15 billion to the annual import bill — all sourced from the rupee market.

3

FII / FPI flows

$1B outflow → ~₹0.10 weakness

Foreign portfolio investors hold $700B+ in Indian equities. Their buying-selling decisions move the rupee in real time. In 2026, $21B has already flown out.

4

Dollar Index (DXY)

DXY up → rupee down

The DXY measures the dollar against six major currencies. When the dollar is strong globally, the rupee weakens regardless of India's own fundamentals. Currently at ~98.3.

5

India's current account deficit

Wider deficit → more dollar demand

India imports more than it exports — the gap must be financed in dollars. A widening current account deficit means more structural dollar buying every month, regardless of news flow.

6

RBI intervention

Sells dollars → slows rupee fall

The Reserve Bank doesn't target a fixed rate but smooths volatility. It sells dollars from $728B in reserves. Effective short-term, but every dollar spent is a dollar not available for a future crisis.

THE OLD MONEY · WHY "64 ANNA" WASN'T QUITE RIGHT

Before 1957 the rupee wasn't decimal. It split into 16 annas, and each anna into 4 pice (or 12 pies) — so 1 rupee = 16 anna = 64 pice = 192 pies. In 1957 India switched to 100 naye paise ("new paise") to the rupee. That's the real "64 → 100" jump: the base changed from a 64-pice system to a clean 100-paise decimal one, making arithmetic — and trade — far simpler.

Sources Anna (currency) Paisa · decimalization
SO — IS THE DOLLAR NEXT?

Every previous crown fell eventually, but never quickly and never without a ready replacement. The dollar still settles ~88% of FX trades and holds ~58% of reserves; the yuan is under 3%. History says dominance is temporary — but it also says the successor takes decades to arrive. For the rupee, the lesson is narrow: you don't have to replace the dollar to stop being squeezed by it. You just have to need it a little less.

THE TRAP FOR IMPORTERS

India earns rupees. The world charges dollars for oil.

To pay that bill, India has exactly three ways to source dollars. Each one has a failure mode. When two or three fail together — like today — the rupee has nowhere to go but down.

STEP 2 · INDIA DOWNSTREAM

How the petrodollar loop reaches the rupee.

01
Households pay more

Fuel at the pump, gold, cooking oil, mobile phones — anything sourced from abroad immediately costs more rupees. Inflation rises even when domestic demand is weak.

02
Companies with dollar debt suffer

Indian firms that borrowed in dollars must repay more rupees per dollar. Interest costs rise mechanically. Refinancing becomes expensive even if rates haven't moved.

03
RBI burns the war chest

To slow the slide, the Reserve Bank sells dollars from its reserves. Effective in the short run, but every dollar spent is a dollar not available for a future shock.

04
The current account widens

Imports get costlier in rupee terms while exports don't immediately surge. The trade gap grows, which means even more dollar demand next month. The loop reinforces itself.

+
One offset: dollar earners gain

TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Sun Pharma — companies billing in dollars convert back to more rupees. Their earnings rise mechanically with rupee weakness. This is why the Nifty can hold up while households feel pain.

→
India's slow workaround

Rupee invoicing with Russia and the UAE, BRICS payment experiments, and direct-rupee trade arrangements all chip at the petrodollar's edges. None reverse the system. But over a decade, they accumulate into a real alternative.

Foreign investors withdraw at the same time, and a strong dollar globally amplifies each force. The rupee has no buffer that can absorb all four at once.

THE PLUMBING · WHO SITS WHERE

The dollar isn't just a currency. It's the world's plumbing — and every country sits somewhere on the pipe.

Think of dollars as water in a single pipe. It's pumped in at the source and flows downhill. Where a country sits on that pipe decides how much power it has — and how hard it gets squeezed when the pressure drops. India sits near the bottom. Here's the whole run, top to bottom.

  1. SOURCE
    The tap

    🇺🇸 United States

    Prints the water everyone downstream needs. Every barrel of oil, most cross-border debt and ~58% of central-bank reserves are denominated in its currency, so demand never runs dry.

    Leverage: sets the weather for everyone
  2. UPSTREAM
    The reservoir keepers

    🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia · 🇦🇪 UAE · 🇶🇦 Qatar

    Sell oil, collect dollars they can't spend fast enough, and choose where to park the surplus — mostly back into US Treasuries. They hold real leverage: they decide whether the loop keeps closing.

    Leverage: can choose to price oil elsewhere
  3. MIDSTREAM
    The big buyers

    🇪🇺 Eurozone · 🇯🇵 Japan

    Large importers that also issue reserve-grade currencies (euro, yen). They pay dollars for oil, but the world holds their money too — so a wobble doesn't threaten their survival. A cushion India doesn't have.

    Leverage: own a partial alternative currency
  4. DOWNSTREAM
    The squeezed

    🇮🇳 India

    Earns rupees, but must buy dollars for oil, debt and most imports. No reserve currency, 85% of crude imported, and four pressures firing at once. When flow slows, India feels it first and worst.

    Leverage: low — the rupee absorbs the shock
    ₹95.96 = $1 · all-time low
THE WORKAROUND · HOW CHINA AND RUSSIA ROUTE AROUND THE DOLLAR

Two countries stopped waiting downstream and dug their own pipe.

India mostly plays inside the dollar system. China and Russia are the two large economies actively building an alternative — for very different reasons. Neither has replaced the dollar. But they show what "sitting downstream" doesn't have to look like.

🇨🇳
China — building a parallel system

Position: a huge importer that refuses to stay downstream.

  • CIPS. Its own cross-border payment rail, an alternative to the US-linked SWIFT/dollar plumbing.
  • Yuan-priced oil. Buys crude from Russia, Iran and increasingly the Gulf settled partly in yuan, chipping at oil's dollar monopoly.
  • Swap lines & the digital yuan. Dozens of central-bank currency swaps and the e-CNY let trade partners hold and settle yuan directly.
  • The catch. The yuan isn't freely convertible and capital controls scare off reserve managers, so it's still <3% of global reserves.
Strategy: patient, state-built alternative rails
🇷🇺
Russia — forced off the pipe

Position: an exporter cut off from dollar plumbing by sanctions.

  • Sanctions shock. After 2022, frozen reserves and SWIFT cutoffs made dollars/euros unusable — de-dollarization became survival, not choice.
  • Yuan & rupee settlement. Sells oil for yuan and rupees; the rupee balances piled up so fast they became a problem to spend.
  • Gold & national currencies. Shifted reserves into gold and yuan, and prices energy in "friendly" currencies.
  • The catch. Trapped rupee revenue and a thin yuan market show how clumsy life is once you're pushed off the dollar rail.
Strategy: improvised exit under sanctions pressure
WHERE THIS LEAVES INDIA

India sits between the two: too integrated with the West to break away like Russia, too dependent on imported capital to go it alone like China. Its play is quieter — rupee invoicing with Russia and the UAE, local-currency trade deals, and bigger reserves — chipping at the edges rather than digging a whole new pipe. That's why the rupee slides while it waits.

HOW TO READ THIS

Don't read the map by size or borders — read it by who needs whose money. Two things are moving: the flows (what travels between countries) and the roles (where each country sits in the loop).

The three flows
Oil — physical crude moving from exporters to importers, always with a dollar price tag.
Dollars — printed by the US and sent out into the world as loans, investment and payment.
Recycling — exporters' dollar surpluses flowing back into US Treasuries, closing the loop.
The four roles
Issuer — prints the currency everyone else needs. Sets the weather for the whole system.
Exporter / recycler — earns the surplus and chooses where to park it. Leverage lives here.
Importer — pays the bill. Its only real defense is settling more trade outside the dollar.
The squeezed — earns rupees, pays for essentials in dollars. Where India sits, and why the rupee slides.

KNOWLEDGE BASE

The terms, the questions, and where to go deeper.

This page uses a fair amount of jargon. Here's a plain-English glossary of the key terms, answers to the questions the story naturally raises, and a short reading list if you want to go past the summary.

No terms match "". Try a broader word — e.g. oil, Fed, or reserves.

GLOSSARY
Petrodollar
Dollars that oil-exporting countries earn by pricing and selling crude in US dollars — then "recycle" back into US assets, mainly Treasuries. The arrangement dates to the 1970s and is the engine that keeps global demand for dollars high.
Reserve currency
The currency other countries hold in their central-bank reserves and use to settle international trade and debt. The dollar is ~58% of global reserves, which is why the world keeps absorbing new dollars instead of dumping them.
Quantitative easing (QE) & QT
QE is the Fed creating new reserves to buy bonds, expanding the dollar supply without printing physical cash. QT (tightening) is the reverse — letting the balance sheet shrink. QE tends to push dollars into emerging markets like India; QT pulls them back out.
M2 money supply
A broad measure of dollars in circulation — cash, checking and savings deposits, and money-market funds. US M2 grew from $4.9T in 2000 to $22.7T in 2026. It's the "how much money exists" number the page tracks against the rupee.
Dollar Index (DXY)
A measure of the dollar's strength against a basket of six major currencies (euro, yen, pound, and others). When the DXY rises, the dollar is strong globally — and the rupee tends to weaken regardless of India's own fundamentals.
Carry trade
Borrowing cheaply in a low-rate currency (often the dollar or yen) to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere — Indian bonds and equities, for example. It floods money in when conditions are calm and yanks it out the moment risk rises.
FPI / FII flows
Foreign Portfolio (or Institutional) Investment — money overseas investors put into Indian stocks and bonds. It's "hot" money that can leave fast: their buying and selling moves the rupee in real time. India saw ~$21B of outflows in 2026.
Current account deficit
When a country imports more goods, services and income than it exports. India runs one structurally because of its oil bill — and the gap must be financed in dollars, creating steady dollar demand that pressures the rupee.
Taper tantrum
The 2013 episode when the Fed merely hinted it would slow QE, and capital fled emerging markets. The rupee crashed from ₹54 to ₹68 in months — the textbook example of how sensitive India is to Fed signals.
De-dollarization
The slow effort to settle trade — especially oil — outside the dollar, using local currencies and alternative payment rails (CIPS, mBridge, BRICS Pay, rupee-vostro accounts). Real but gradual; it shows up in plumbing years before headlines.
RBI intervention
The Reserve Bank of India selling dollars from its reserves to slow the rupee's fall. It smooths volatility rather than targeting a fixed rate — but every dollar spent is one not available for a future crisis.
QUESTIONS
Why can't India just print rupees to pay for oil?
Because Saudi Arabia and most exporters want dollars, not rupees. India has to first sell rupees to buy dollars on the market — so printing more rupees would only weaken the currency further and make oil more expensive, not less.
If the US prints so much, why doesn't the dollar collapse?
Because the world needs dollars to buy oil, settle trade and hold reserves. That standing demand absorbs the extra supply. A normal currency weakens when you print it; the dollar is propped up by being everyone else's plumbing.
Is a weaker rupee always bad?
Not entirely. It makes imports and foreign travel costlier for households, but it boosts exporters — IT and pharma firms earning dollars get more rupees per dollar. It's why the stock market can hold up even as the currency slides.
Is de-dollarization actually happening?
Slowly, and at the margins. Russia–India oil trades in rupees and rubles, the UAE runs rupee-settled deals, and China pushes yuan settlement. But the dollar is still ~90% of FX trades — the shift is real but measured in decades, not years.
Can the RBI just stop the rupee from falling?
Only temporarily. It can sell dollars to smooth sharp moves, and it holds a large buffer to do so. But fighting a structural trend burns reserves fast — so the RBI manages the pace of depreciation rather than preventing it.
What would actually strengthen the rupee?
No single lever — a stack of them: de-dollarizing oil trade, growing non-oil exports, attracting sticky long-term investment over hot money, and a deeper reserve buffer. The "Way Out" section lets you test how far each goes.

Methodology & notes

  • Exchange rates use the RBI Reference Rate (FBIL) rounded to two decimals. Intraday market rates may differ.
  • M2 figures are seasonally adjusted, drawn from the FRED series M2SL maintained by the St. Louis Fed.
  • FPI / FII data aggregates equity and debt flows reported by NSDL and CDSL through May 14, 2026.
  • Crude prices reference Brent front-month ICE futures, used because India sources crude from a basket benchmarked to Brent and Dubai.
  • Historical milestones (2008 QE1, 2013 taper, 2020 COVID, 2022 hikes) are sourced from Federal Reserve press releases and FOMC minutes.
  • All percentage moves are computed from underlying series, not rounded inputs, then displayed to one or two decimals.
